## How do you purify your investments?

Some halal stocks still earn a small amount of income from interest. AAOIFI allows up to 5% of a company's income from prohibited sources, but you must give away the part that belongs to your shares. (AAOIFI Standard 21, clause 3/4/6)

The steps: 1) Find the company's prohibited income for its financial year. 2) Divide it by the number of shares to get the amount per share. 3) Multiply by the shares you own. (3/4/6/4)

Shortcut at today's price: your holding value x (prohibited income / market cap). Example: AED 10,000 in a company where that ratio is 0.12% means about AED 12 to give away.

That money is given away, not kept, and you may not benefit from it yourself, including to pay taxes. (3/4/6/5) Screening is informational, not a fatwa.

Basis: AAOIFI

## Who has to purify, and when?

Whoever owns the share at the end of the company's financial period, whether they are a long-term investor or a trader. If you sold before the period ended, you do not owe it for that period. (AAOIFI Standard 21, clause 3/4/6/1)

It is due even if the company paid no dividend, and whether it made a profit or a loss. (3/4/6/2)

## Where does the purified money go?

AAOIFI says the purified amount may not bring any benefit to you, including paying your taxes. (AAOIFI Standard 21, clause 3/4/6/5) The usual route is giving it away to charity.

Which charity or cause is a question for your scholar. Safr does not choose for you, and Safr does not collect or hold any money. For a ruling on your situation, please ask a qualified scholar.

## Do I purify if I only hold, and the company pays no dividend?

Yes. Purification does not depend on dividends. It is due if you owned the share at the end of the company's financial period, even with no dividend and even if the company made a loss. (AAOIFI Standard 21, clause 3/4/6/2)

Basis: AAOIFI

## Why is a stock with some interest income still halal?

AAOIFI sets limits rather than demanding zero: prohibited income up to 5% of total income (clause 3/4/4), borrowing up to 30% of market cap (3/4/2), and interest-bearing deposits up to 30% (3/4/3). A company inside all the limits and with a permissible business passes, and the shareholder purifies their share of the prohibited income. (3/4/6)

Other standard-setters use different limits, which is why scholars and screeners can disagree. Safr follows AAOIFI. Screening is informational, not a fatwa.

Basis: AAOIFI

## How can a stock be halal?

A stock represents a share in a company. It can pass the halal checks when its main business is permissible and its finances also pass.

We look at what the company does, its borrowing, money held in interest-bearing accounts, and income from prohibited sources. A prohibited business or financial figures above the screening limits means it does not pass.

When figures are missing, Safr says that part is unverified. Passing the checks does not remove investment risk. Some income may still need purification.

Screening is informational, not a fatwa.

## What is halal investing?

In general: it means investing in businesses and assets that fit Islamic principles. That avoids interest (riba), excessive uncertainty (gharar), gambling (maysir), and businesses such as alcohol, pork, conventional banking and gambling.

Safr checks stocks against AAOIFI's screening rules and shows you the reason. Screening is informational, not a fatwa.

## What is riba?

In general: riba is interest, or any guaranteed extra return charged on money lent. Islamic finance treats it as prohibited, so returns should come from real assets, trade or shared risk instead.

This is why Safr screens how much interest a company pays and earns. For a ruling on your situation, please ask a qualified scholar.

## What is a sukuk?

In general: a sukuk is a certificate of ownership in an asset or project. Investors get a share of the returns the asset earns, not interest on a loan. That is why sukuk are the halal alternative to bonds.

In Safr's model mix, shorter goals lean more on sukuk. Specific sukuk are not screened in this prototype.

## What is takaful, and how is it halal?

In general: takaful is Islamic insurance. Here is how it works, step by step.

1) You contribute. Members pay into a shared pool, treated as a gift to help each other.
2) The pool pays claims. If a member suffers a covered loss, the claim is paid from the pool, not from the company's own money.
3) The operator manages it. It runs the pool for a fee, and does not own the risk.
4) The money is invested only in Shariah-compliant assets, with no interest.
5) A Shariah board oversees the product. Any surplus can be shared back with members.

Why it is considered halal: conventional insurance is a sale of uncertainty for a premium and usually invests in interest. Takaful replaces that with shared responsibility and permissible investments.

In Safr's journey it is the protect step, before you invest. Safr has no partnership with any provider. For your own situation, please ask a qualified scholar.

Basis: General

## How much zakat do I give on my investments?

In general: the rate usually discussed is 2.5% of the value of your zakatable wealth, once your total is above the nisab (a minimum, based on 85 grams of gold) and you have held it for a lunar year.

For shares, scholars differ. A common view is that shares you hold to trade are valued at market price, so 2.5% of their value. Shares you hold long term for income are often treated differently, based on the company's zakatable assets. AAOIFI's zakah standard (No. 35) is written for Islamic financial institutions, not individuals, so it is only a guide.

Example only: AED 100,000 of shares held to trade, with total wealth above the nisab and held for a lunar year, would be 2.5% = AED 2,500. Your own amount depends on your situation. Safr does not calculate your zakat. For your own amount, please ask a qualified scholar.

Basis: General

## What is gharar?

In general: gharar is excessive uncertainty in a contract, where the terms, price or subject are so unclear that someone can be harmed or cheated. Islamic finance avoids it, which is why contracts must be clear about what is being bought and sold.
